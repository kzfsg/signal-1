This is the thesis as I understand it:

Pre-seed fundraising has a qualification problem, not merely an outreach problem. Founders can find investors and book meetings through manual outreach, mass outreach, warm introductions, or tools like Boardy—but profiles and websites rarely reveal enough to know whether both sides are genuinely aligned.

Because each side holds back context, they default to a 30–60 minute coffee chat to discover basic compatibility:

- Does the investor actually invest at this stage?
- Do they understand the founder’s category?
- Are check size, ownership expectations, and timing compatible?
- Do founder and investor share working style and values?
- Can this investor provide the specific help the founder needs?
- Is either party genuinely interested right now?

Signal compresses that exploratory conversation. Each person gives a private agent their goals, constraints, values, and disclosure boundaries. The agents conduct the “first coffee,” exchange only permitted information, and return:

- a clear alignment assessment,
- supporting evidence and notes,
- unresolved questions,
- and a recommendation to meet, defer, or decline.

The promise is not “schedule more investor meetings.” It is:

> **Only spend human time on conversations that deserve human time.**

That fits the hackathon theme extremely well. The strongest demonstration would show a founder’s crowded fundraising calendar being reduced to a handful of high-alignment meetings, while preserving warm relationships with everyone else.

The pivotal product decision is: **must each investor create their own Signal agent, or should a founder be able to qualify investors who have not joined Signal yet?**

I’ve read your notes. The central idea is that **Signal learns what makes a meeting worthwhile for each person, then uses conversation to discover whether that alignment exists.** The definition of a good match can change as the founder, company, or investor’s priorities change.

Your interviews give us useful distinctions. Alike’s experience suggests a large meeting burden and a learning curve around qualifying investors and running a raise. Darwin’s account suggests outreach itself was manageable; understanding the partner’s background, portfolio, thesis, and current interest mattered more. These are qualitative observations from your interviews—we don’t yet have evidence for a general claim that a particular percentage of coffee chats goes nowhere.

My earlier framing was too rigid: stage, sector, check size, and a meet/skip verdict only capture part of what you mean.

A useful definition of a “good signal” would be **evidence that this particular relationship could advance something each person currently cares about, on terms they can accept.** That includes several kinds of information:

- **Practical compatibility:** stage, check size, timing, geography, and investment capacity.
- **Personal preferences:** domain experience, involvement, founder autonomy, communication style, and the kind of support desired.
- **Trade-offs:** someone might accept a smaller check for strong expertise, or more involvement from a partner they trust.
- **Unknowns:** questions that need clarification or a human conversation before reaching a conclusion.

The conversation needs to turn broad preferences into concrete expectations. For example, “I want freedom” is ambiguous. An agent could establish whether that means product decisions, hiring, board control, or time to pursue research. If the investor wants “active involvement,” the next question should explore what that means in practice. Those statements might be compatible; comparing the labels alone would miss that.

The agent should also recognize when it lacks an answer and return to its person. Otherwise it risks inventing preferences to make the conversation continue. The output should explain where the two sides align, where they differ, and what remains unresolved—with evidence behind each conclusion.

The personal context layer is central to this. Your vision is that Signal draws on the work people already do with their assistants: diligence, company planning, reflections on meetings, and changing fundraising priorities. That context can build a living understanding rather than requiring people to reconstruct themselves in a questionnaire.

I understand Muse, Claude, ChatGPT, and Codex as potential connection points to investigate. We haven’t established what context each exposes or how integration would work. Product-wise, the important distinction is between **what the agent knows privately and what it may share with another person’s agent**. Remembered information also needs a source, freshness, and a way for the person to correct it.

For measuring success, I’d start with whether people say a recommended meeting was worthwhile for their stated goal, alongside time spent reaching that decision. We should also examine declined recommendations so the system doesn’t appear successful merely by filtering out nearly everything.

That’s the product understanding I’ll carry forward: personalized, evolving, mutual alignment, discovered through evidence-seeking conversation. The existing demo captures the calendar-saving story; its fixed criteria and scripted outcomes don’t yet express this deeper behavior.
